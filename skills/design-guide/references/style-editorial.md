# Editorial design

Use with the shared foundations. Compose a clear reading experience with the judgement of an editor: what leads, what supports it and what the reader needs next.

## Character and fit

Useful for publications, substantial case studies, cultural organisations, curated commerce and content-led services. The audience situation is willingness to read, compare or explore meaningful material; a magazine appearance alone does not establish readership or trust.

The main costs are strong copy, imagery, a repeatable content model and responsive composition. Avoid borrowing only giant serif headlines and arbitrary overlapping images. An editorial system needs a rhythm that survives the next article.

## Composition, type and colour rules

- Define an underlying grid, reading measure and alignment anchors. Break the grid in a few intentional places, while captions and reading columns provide continuity. Asymmetry needs a visible relationship, not random offsets.
- Establish roles for title, standfirst, section heading, body, caption, byline and metadata when those roles exist. Distinguish hierarchy through size, weight, spacing and placement before introducing another typeface.
- Sequence the content around the reader's question. Choose a leading story/image, explanatory material and a next action. Long-form pages may need an index, section anchors or related content; add them for navigation needs, not to imitate a magazine.
- Alternate feature scale with supporting content density. Define reusable article/index patterns so every item does not require a bespoke art-directed layout. A major feature may break the pattern while standard entries remain predictable.
- Let photography/illustration and typography carry much of the colour. Use rules, section marks or an accent to organise, with a stable reading surface. Paper-like warmth is optional, not an obligatory beige palette.
- Specify image aspect ratios, focal crops and caption placement per content role. Do not crop away evidence, faces or relevant product details simply to make a dramatic spread. Associate captions with the correct image semantically and visually.

## All six techniques

| Technique | Concrete application |
|---|---|
| Anchor typography | Give titles a distinctive editorial voice; provide comfortable body type and clearly subordinate metadata. Check italics, numerals, links and required scripts. Serif/sans pairing is an option, not a rule. |
| Star of the show | Lead with the story, photograph or comparison that best answers the visitor's question. Record how its title, image and standfirst form one focal group instead of competing as separate heroes. |
| Visual rhyming | Repeat caption styling, section numbers, column rules or framing in at least two named locations. Use a common baseline/alignment where it improves rhythm without forcing fragile positioning. |
| Subtle depth | Use a paper-toned plane, restrained grain outside reading areas or one purposeful image overlap. Keep reading text on a stable surface; depth must not obscure the editorial sequence. |
| Text opacity and emphasis | Distinguish title, standfirst, body and caption roles through semantic tokens. Captions and dates can recede in hierarchy without becoming too faint to read. |
| Design variations | Compare a cover-led opening with an index-led arrangement or asymmetric feature grid using the same stories. Judge finding, reading and onward action, not just the first-screen screenshot. |

## Components and content system

Use semantic headings, article regions, figures/captions and lists where appropriate. Visual order must agree with meaningful document and keyboard order; CSS reordering must not make a screen reader encounter conclusions before context.

Make article cards predictable: title, image and metadata have defined roles, and the link target is clear. Do not create nested interactive links/buttons inside a fully clickable container. Preserve text links and visible focus. Reuse the incumbent reading/navigation components rather than inventing new ones for each story.

Record rules for missing imagery, long titles, multiple authors and absent metadata. Do not invent quotes, authors or dates to fill a layout. Pull quotes may visually repeat content but should not create confusing repeated announcements. Preserve reading position when images/fonts load.

## Mobile, motion and delivery

Recompose a spread into a deliberate sequence of title, context, media, caption and body. Do not simply shrink columns. Keep image/caption pairs together and retain useful section navigation. Localise layouts with real Thai copy; allow enough room for marks and longer headings instead of forcing a Latin magazine measure.

Use motion sparingly to connect content or feedback. Body reading and essential links remain stable. Reduced motion reveals everything in the same logical sequence. Lazy-load below-the-fold media without delaying the leading content or moving the reading column unexpectedly.

## Failure patterns and review

| Reject | Verify instead |
|---|---|
| Every block is a different layout | A small content model explains repeatable article/index patterns |
| Desktop beauty becomes scrambled mobile order | Narrow layout and document order tell the same story |
| Huge titles dominate weak or missing content | Real copy, media, captions and onward action support the hierarchy |
| Texture sits beneath all body text | Reading contrast and comfortable scanning survive the material treatment |

Possible project rules: preserve the body measure and caption pairing; restrict overlaps to feature media; convert the specified spread into a named mobile sequence. Review at least one long article and one dense index if both are in scope.
