# Laws of UX article synthesis

This reference is an original operational synthesis of the English articles listed at `https://lawsofux.com/articles/`, authored by Jon Yablonski and accessed on 25 August 2026. It preserves the practical lessons without reproducing the articles.

## Familiar vs Novel

Source: `https://lawsofux.com/articles/2024/familiar-vs-novel/`

- Familiar patterns let users transfer existing mental models and focus on their task rather than the interface.
- Novelty is justified when it creates meaningful differentiation, enables a genuinely new capability, or supports exploration and surprise.
- Commodity tasks such as buying, signing in, navigating, and confirming should usually remain recognisable.
- Novel interactions require direct research and usability testing with the intended audience.
- Reject novelty when its memorability is purchased with poorer task success or accessibility.

## Onboarding for Active Users

Source: `https://lawsofux.com/articles/2024/onboarding-for-active-users/`

- Many users begin acting before reading instructions, known as the Paradox of the Active User.
- Make guidance available throughout the product instead of forcing a long product tour before value.
- Prefer contextual tooltips, progressive introduction, safe practice, useful templates, and checklists tied to a real task.
- Guidance must be discoverable but not intrusive. Too many prompts become another layer of friction.
- Validate time to first value, independent success, feature discovery, and whether users can recover after skipping guidance.

## Tesler's Law

Source: `https://lawsofux.com/articles/2024/teslers-law/`

- Every system contains some irreducible complexity. Design decides whether the user or the system carries it.
- Absorb repeatable complexity through defaults, remembered context, prediction, automation, and integration.
- Avoid oversimplification that hides necessary control, cause, consequence, or state.
- Progressive disclosure can manage complexity, but buried essentials create discovery debt.
- Measure task effort, errors, reversals, and whether automation behaves predictably.

## UX Psychology: Google Search

Source: `https://lawsofux.com/articles/2020/ux-psychology-google-search/`

- Make the first intent obvious and ready for input with minimal competing choices.
- Predictions and preserved queries reduce typing and working-memory demand.
- Fast response protects attention, but the interface must still communicate honest status.
- Chunked results, spacing, proximity, and connected regions make dense information scannable.
- Simplicity can expand after the first action. Entry and refinement need different levels of choice.

## Peak-End Rule

Source: `https://lawsofux.com/articles/2020/peak-end-rule/`

- People often remember an experience disproportionately through its most intense moment and its ending.
- Map emotional peaks, anxiety, success, failure, recovery, and final confirmation across the journey.
- Reinforce real accomplishment and make the outcome and next step unmistakable.
- Errors and outages can become recovery opportunities, but humour or brand voice must not obscure severity.
- Fix destructive peaks before adding celebration. Validate later recall as well as immediate reaction.

## The Psychology of Design

Source: `https://lawsofux.com/articles/2018/the-psychology-of-design/`

- Align interfaces with how people perceive, decide, remember, and transfer prior knowledge.
- Hick's Law helps diagnose unstructured choice; cognitive load and working memory explain avoidable mental work; chunking improves meaningful grouping; Jakob's Law supports familiar conventions.
- A law must serve the target user's goal rather than force the user to adapt to the designer's model.
- Familiarity and innovation require judgement, not blind loyalty to either side.
- User well-being and agency outrank addictive engagement patterns.

## Designing with Occam's Razor

Source: `https://lawsofux.com/articles/2017/designing-with-occams-razor/`

- Start with the minimum interface that lets content be found and the task be completed.
- Add complexity only when it explains, enables, protects, or recovers something material.
- Review every element for purpose and remove attachment-driven ornament.
- Hidden navigation and decorative animation can create more work than they remove.
- Use critique to force explicit reasoning and expose unnecessary assumptions.

## Design Principles for Reducing Cognitive Load

Source: `https://lawsofux.com/articles/2015/design-principles-for-reducing-cognitive-load/`

- Remove elements and tasks that do not help the user's goal, but do not trade clarity for visual emptiness.
- Use familiar patterns so the user can spend attention on the task rather than learning controls.
- Reuse known information, set safe defaults, and avoid unnecessary reading, remembering, and deciding.
- Prioritise and group choices rather than scattering or concealing them.
- Use readable typography and label unfamiliar icons. Recognition is cheaper than recall.
- Validate comprehension, task success, errors, and backtracking rather than relying on perceived simplicity.

## Cross-article operating principles

1. Familiar core, purposeful novelty.
2. Learn through doing, with contextual guidance.
3. Put repeatable complexity in the system.
4. Reduce extraneous load while preserving meaningful choice and control.
5. Make status, hierarchy, and grouping visible.
6. Fix the journey before decorating its peak or ending.
7. Treat every law as a hypothesis that must survive user evidence.
